# Session 13 Hiring manager conversation and story preparation

## Scope and coach setup

Prepare the L5 Developer Platform manager conversation after the first DSA round. This is individual-contributor interview preparation. The recruiter explicitly named projects and experience, stack, interest in Netflix, the culture memo, examples of personal involvement in complex work, and questions for the manager.

Read [TUTOR.md](../../learning-material/TUTOR.md), [manager preparation progress](../study-progress.json), this session, the separate [research chapter](../reading/13-hiring-manager.md), and the [story selector](../story-bank.md). Read the selected card under `../stories/`, the [gaps checklist](../gaps.md), and [culture framing](../culture-framing.md) as relevant. Historical evidence is in `../notes/story-evidence.md`; it is not the default reading path. Retrieve them yourself. The research chapter contains sources, 18 question families, role-specific prompts and answer ingredients. Do not infer the candidate's work history from the Go exercises. The posting includes multiple teams; use a confirmed team if later supplied.

For a preparation request, start with story development. For an explicit mock, ask realistic questions, withhold coaching until the learner answers, and use follow-ups based on that answer. There is no timer, editor or required written homework. Default to one question per turn; honor the learner’s request for numbered batches during story discovery. The rounds below are selectable coaching steps, not a script that must be completed in order.

Explain that a good story makes a decision assessable: the problem, the candidate's role, available alternatives, actual actions, consequences and reflection. Explain only the missing element before asking for it. Do not give an ideal answer and immediately ask the learner to repeat it.

## Suggested opening

Explain in natural language: “The manager needs to understand what you did and how you made decisions. We can begin with one project, find the important events inside it, and build an answer from the facts. You do not need a polished story yet. For this role, it helps to explain whose work improved and how you know.”

Ask only the question in round 1. If a previous story is already in progress, resume its saved next action instead.

## Round 1 Find a relevant project

**Question:** Which project have you enjoyed that would best show the work you could bring to this role?

**Coach explanation:** A useful example has a real user or customer, a meaningful difficulty and enough personal involvement to support follow-up. It need not involve Netflix-like scale or a platform job title. Internal automation, a migration, an operational improvement or a customer-facing service may transfer well.

**Listen for:** What the project achieved and why it mattered to the learner. If the answer is only a technology list, ask next who used the result and what problem they had. Record candidate facts before proposing wording.

## Round 2 Make ownership precise

**Question:** What were you personally responsible for on that project?

**Coach explanation:** Team success and personal contribution can both be clear. Name the decision or work the candidate owned, collaborators' contributions, and the boundary of their authority. Leadership can mean coordinating a change without managing anyone.

**Listen for:** Specific verbs such as designed, implemented, diagnosed, negotiated or decided, each connected to an actual event. If “we” conceals scope, ask for one decision they made. Do not turn normal collaboration into an inflated solo achievement.

## Round 3 Explain a difficult choice

**Question:** What was the most consequential technical choice you made on this project?

**Coach explanation:** Depth comes from requirements, alternatives and consequences. A credible decision has an accepted cost and conditions under which another choice would have been better. Inherited choices can still reveal judgment through how the learner evaluated or changed them.

**Listen for:** Evidence available at the time and a real tradeoff. Probe one of the rejected options next. If unfamiliar stack details arise, follow the repository's documentation rules for any library-specific explanation; do not bluff about API behaviour.

## Round 4 Establish the outcome

**Question:** What evidence showed whether this project helped its users?

**Coach explanation:** A number is useful when its baseline, scope and period are understood. Direct customer observation, fewer repeated failures or successful completion of a previously blocked workflow can also be evidence. Delivery and benefit are different claims.

**Listen for:** Supported results and acknowledged limits. If a number is uncertain, record the uncertainty. Ask next about possible alternative causes or an outcome that did not improve. Never invent a percentage to strengthen the story.

## Round 5 Understand adoption and collaboration

**Question:** What was the hardest part of getting another team to use or migrate to your solution?

**Coach explanation:** Adoption creates work and risk for the other team. Strong evidence includes discovering their constraints, adjusting the product or transition, supporting a pilot, and handling exceptions. A mandate is background; the learner's contribution is what made the change workable.

**Listen for:** The other team's perspective and an actual interaction. If the answer only discusses migration code, ask next how a partner's feedback changed the plan. If no such event exists, choose a different collaboration story.

## Round 6 Explore disagreement

**Question:** Tell me about a decision on which you and a colleague had a substantial disagreement.

**Coach explanation:** Reconstruct both positions fairly. The important evidence is how the disagreement was handled and how the decision became possible. The learner need not be the person whose proposal won.

**Listen for:** Direct discussion, evidence, listening and a clear decision owner. A useful follow-up is what the colleague would say about the disagreement. Distinguish an unresolved difference from a relationship failure.

## Round 7 Examine independent judgment

**Question:** Tell me about an important decision you made when the available information was incomplete.

**Coach explanation:** Independence includes gathering relevant context and deciding what uncertainty can be tolerated. Clarify authority, the downside, how others were informed, and what would cause a reversal. Do not equate consultation with weakness or acting alone with seniority.

**Listen for:** A real decision the learner owned. If the example was delegated execution, help find another event. If the decision later changed, explore the new evidence without treating that as an automatic failure.

## Round 8 Work through feedback received

**Question:** What critical feedback led you to change how you work?

**Coach explanation:** Identify the behaviour, why it affected someone, the reaction, and what actually changed. A later event can establish improvement. Acknowledging valid criticism does not require accepting an inaccurate criticism in full.

**Listen for:** A material issue and evidence beyond agreeing in the moment. If it is a disguised compliment, explain the missing evidence and ask for a genuine development area. Do not diagnose personality from a single response.

## Round 9 Work through feedback given

**Question:** Tell me about an uncomfortable piece of feedback you gave a colleague.

**Coach explanation:** Effective feedback makes a problem discussable and gives the recipient something to act on. The setting, relationship, substance, response and follow-up matter. Clarify who was involved without demanding private names.

**Listen for:** Respectful specificity and willingness to hear an explanation. If the learner describes escalation, ask why it was necessary. Never add an unreported conversation with a manager to repair the answer.

## Round 10 Learn from a setback

**Question:** Tell me about a mistake or failed bet for which you had meaningful responsibility.

**Coach explanation:** A useful answer explains the damage or missed outcome, communication, response and changed practice. A failure can concern usefulness, prioritization or adoption as well as an outage. Avoid hindsight that pretends the correct answer was obvious at the time.

**Listen for:** Genuine ownership and a specific lesson. If the learner rescued a problem caused entirely by someone else, explain why that demonstrates recovery but does not yet answer this question. Offer a different example search.

## Round 11 Discuss culture thoughtfully

**Question:** Which idea in Netflix's culture memo would require the most adjustment from you?

**Coach explanation:** An informed answer can connect a real working preference or experience to a question about this team. The goal is honest mutual assessment. Recalling labels or asserting agreement is insufficient to understand how someone works.

**Listen for:** A concrete example, self-awareness and an actionable question. If the learner has not understood the relevant idea, explain it from the research chapter before asking a smaller question. Do not tell them to hide a real concern or fabricate disagreement.

## Round 12 Connect experience to motivation

**Question:** What makes Developer Platform at Netflix a good next step for you?

**Coach explanation:** Connect actual experience and interests to the work. Motivation is clearer when it names a problem the learner wants to solve, what they bring, and what they hope to learn. Keep the exact team assignment conditional until established.

**Listen for:** A role-specific reason with supporting experience. Next, if useful, explore one strength or gap against the role. Conclude preparation by helping select questions whose answers would matter to the learner, drawing from the reading chapter.

## Follow-up selection and feedback

Use the research chapter's Q09 when an example raises a decision the learner opposed, Q14 for priorities, Q15 for inclusion or mentorship, and Q18 for manager expectations. The technical team prompts are optional and should follow the confirmed charter or the learner's actual experience. Do not transform this session into another algorithm round.

After an answer, state the strongest supported evidence, then the single most important missing element. Ask one follow-up. If the learner is stuck, explain the distinction and help retrieve a smaller real event. A hypothetical example may teach the structure but must remain labelled hypothetical.

For an explicit mock, include career or motivation, a project with follow-ups, a working-style event, and the learner's questions. Adapt the mix to their needs. Do not assign a Netflix hiring probability or claim an official pass score.

## Readiness and persistence

Assess whether the story answers the actual question, has understandable context, shows personal scope and judgment, represents other people fairly, supports the outcome, and contains honest reflection. Label each area clear, needs detail or not yet observed. These are coaching observations, not personality ratings.

With write access, save concise facts and proposed outlines in the relevant story card, and keep the story selector and gaps checklist current after substantive exchanges. Preserve the DSA resume point when recording this track in `hiring-manager/study-progress.json` (repository-relative); do not mark previous chapters complete because the DSA round was passed. Save the last question and precise next action. Authoring or reading this session alone does not establish practice or mastery.

With read-only access, maintain a conversation handoff and explicitly state that it has not been saved to GitHub. Never ask the learner to paste files, operate an editor or run code to continue.
